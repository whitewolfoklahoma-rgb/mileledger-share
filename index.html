Why a manual log?

The IRS does not ask for a GPS trail. It asks for a record with the date, the destination, the business purpose, and the miles — which is exactly what MileLedger's trip log captures, on paper-ready reports. The subscription apps track your location around the clock (and drain your battery doing it) to build the same four facts. Here you type them in, your data never leaves this device, and there is no monthly bill.

Sample data (a realtor with two vehicles) is pre-loaded so you can see everything working. One button removes only the samples — your real entries are never touched.

How MileLedger works

The idea

One file, no account, no subscription, and no GPS tracking — ever. You log each work trip with the four facts the IRS asks for, log each fill-up, and MileLedger turns that into a deduction, an MPG history, a per-trip gas cost, and a reimbursement claim. Manual entry is the feature: nothing watches where you drive.

What the IRS wants in a mileage log

For the standard-mileage deduction, the record that holds up is a contemporaneous log showing, for each trip: the date, the destination, the business purpose, and the miles driven — plus the total miles the vehicle was driven in the year if you are asked for it. MileLedger's Trip Log captures exactly those fields (purpose is a required field on purpose), and the Deductions tab prints them as a clean year log for your preparer or your files. This is a summary of the record-keeping idea, not tax advice — the IRS publication for your situation (commonly Pub. 463 for travel and vehicle expenses) is the authority.

Commuting

Driving between home and a regular workplace is commuting, and commuting miles are generally not deductible. MileLedger logs commutes (they matter for your true cost of working) but excludes them from the deduction — unless you tick the per-trip override for a case you believe qualifies (a temporary work location is the classic example). The override is deliberate, per-trip, and stays visible on the trip and the printed log. When in doubt, ask a tax professional before overriding.

The formulas

Deduction = deductible miles × the standard mileage rate saved for the trip's year (Settings). Deductible miles = Business trips + commutes with the override on. Personal trips never count.

MPG (full-tank method) = miles driven since the previous fill-up ÷ gallons bought at this fill-up. Fill the tank every time or the number wobbles. Fuel cost per mile = fuel spend over measured intervals ÷ miles in those intervals.

Trip gas cost = trip miles ÷ MPG × gas price per gallon. It is the fuel-only cost of a trip — the standard mileage rate exists precisely because a car costs more than gas (tires, oil, wear, depreciation), which is why the deduction is usually larger than the gas cost.

Reimbursement = claimable miles × your employer's rate (Settings). Claimable miles mirror the deduction's work-trip definition (Business + overridden commutes). Reimbursement and deduction are two views of the same miles: if an employer reimburses you under an accountable plan, those miles generally are not also deductible by you. MileLedger shows both totals so you and your preparer can apply the rule — it does not decide it for you.

Your data

Everything is stored in this browser's local storage on this device only. Clearing your browser's site data erases it — export backups from Settings & Backup and keep the file somewhere safe. A backup from this app can be re-imported into any copy of MileLedger.

Defaults to verify

The standard mileage rates and the fallback rate are planning defaults, clearly labeled and editable in Settings. The IRS sets the standard mileage rate by notice; verify yours before filing. The employer reimbursement rate and the gas price are your own inputs.
